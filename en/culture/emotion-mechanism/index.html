How Music Moves Us — Expectation and Resolution

Music carries no feelings — it builds expectations, then keeps, delays or breaks them

Related

Harmonic FunctionCadence

Music carries no emotion itself — no "happy molecules" live in audio. The mainstream account of how it moves us is a system of expectation: music keeps building predictions of "what comes next", then fulfils, delays, intensifies or breaks them.

The skeleton of expectation theory

Taking Western tonal music as the sample (a repertoire that builds expectation with exceptional precision):

  • Build: a progression makes you expect the next chord (the classic: a dominant "wants" to go home);
  • Fulfil: arrival as promised — stable, exhaled (the cadence);
  • Delay: detours and postponement — tension accumulates (why "crescendo, then sudden quiet" works);
  • Break: surprise chords — shock, freshness.

Emotion lives not in the notes but in the gap between notes and prediction. Which explains the double edge of familiarity: too familiar bores (no gap); too foreign confuses (no prediction).

The body joins in

Studies observe music driving physiological responses — heart rate, breathing, skin conductance — and the famous chills: at certain turning points the body reacts before the mind. Those points are usually exactly where the expectation gap is widest.

Two dimensions and one honest limit

Emotion research uses two axes: valence (happy–sad) and arousal (energised–calm).

What is relatively solid: music systematically manipulates arousal — energising or calming — with reasonable cross-cultural stability. The valence direction (joy vs sorrow) is far more culture-, person- and situation-dependent — the same debate as in why major sounds happy.

A practical corollary

  • To stir: widen the expectation gap (faster, louder, bigger turns);
  • To soothe: shrink it (steady, predictable, fading);
  • For sophistication: satisfy the expectation a little late — not too late; the measure of delay is the craft.
