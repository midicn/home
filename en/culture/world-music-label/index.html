The Problem with the Label "World Music"

A label everyone buys often holds two different things

Related

Genre versus FormMelody

"World music" was one of the most successful labels of the late twentieth century — and one of the most criticised. The problem is not that it points at something real, but that it packs two different things into one bag.

Two things in the bag

1. An industry category: a shelf label in the record business and festival market — "not local pop, but sounds exotic";

2. An object of academic criticism: the criticism itself notes that the label bundles together entirely different traditions, erasing their histories and contexts.

One word: market language in one mouth, critical language in the other. Mixed together, discussion slides off the rails.

What the label actually did

  • Put long-neglected repertoire onto records and radio;
  • Gave festivals and tours a programmable bill;
  • Gave some performers new economic opportunities.

Its positive role cannot be dismissed — inside a purely preservationist frame, much local music would have stayed local.

Its problems are real too

  • "Exotic" is an outside view: the label is usually defined by markets with purchasing power; the traditions inside it have no say;
  • "Tradition" is often reconstructed: "authentic" timbres tuned to meet market expectations can be far from how the music actually sounds locally (example 2 above);
  • History is smoothed away: tunes on the same shelf may come from unrelated traditions, even different eras (see the journeys of instruments).

The decisive timing

"World music" spread in step with the rise of large transnational festivals and the industry. Not a coincidence — it is an industry category serving the market. The academic discussion is relatively settled on this: the label is useful, but its edges were drawn by marketing, not by music.

How to use the word

  • Meaning the industry shelf: it is accurate (that was its job);
  • Meaning an academic object: say which traditions, by what criteria;
  • The worst use is "I just think this is world music" — treating a market label as an aesthetic standard.
