Judging a Good Teacher — and a Good Student

In a trial lesson, listen not to how well they play but to whether they can diagnose you

Related

"Is this teacher good?" is hard to answer, because a good teacher's value does not lie in performing well but in diagnosing well.

One criterion

During a trial lesson, watch which of these the teacher is doing:

  • Demonstrating — playing for you, explaining how the piece should go;
  • Diagnosing — making you play a passage, then saying "here you used the wrist; here it is the fingers".

The diagnosing teacher is the more valuable one. The reason is simple: demonstration only tells you the target; diagnosis tells you the distance. Most people do not lack a target — they lack an accurate estimate of the distance.

The first example makes this concrete: with a student concerto on the stand, the first job of a good teacher is not explaining how hard the piece is, but working out where this student actually is.

What a healthy lesson looks like

A healthy teaching segment usually contains:

1. Listen to you play (all the way through, without interruption); 2. Name a small number of problems (one or two per lesson, not eight); 3. Demonstrate that one spot (to let you hear the difference, not to admire the playing); 4. Have you change it, then listen again (this step is the watershed).

Step 4 is the key. A teacher who only talks and never has you change something has delivered no closed loop.

The "good student" also gets judged

The teacher–student relationship is usually described as one-way delivery, but there is a neglected judgement on the student side:

Are you doing the thing that makes the teacher's diagnosis possible?

Concretely:

  • Asking the same vague question every lesson, "what am I doing wrong", gives the teacher nothing to work with;
  • Never recording, never listening back — a diagnosis without a feedback source is a guess;
  • Practising only what the teacher pointed out, never what you wanted to work on — progress gets narrowed.

The piece from a primary teacher's manual in the third example points at the other face of this: a teacher's professionalism includes knowing when not to teach — when a student needs time alone, the teacher should give time alone.

Concrete things to watch

Directly observable during a trial lesson:

  • Does the teacher let you finish before speaking? Constant interruption means they never hear the whole thing;
  • Are the problems specific about place and action? "Not enough feeling" is not a diagnosis; "wrist too tense at beat 3 of bar 4" is;
  • Is there room to make mistakes? A student afraid to play wrong has nobody willing to correct them;
  • Is the teacher's own demonstration steady? An unsteady demonstration makes you doubt the method;
  • Do you leave with a specific practice list? Without one, the lesson probably did not close.

Signals it is time to change teachers

Not subjective feelings like "they don't like me", but observable things:

  • For three or four weeks running, none of your problems has been named specifically;
  • When you ask "why should it be played that way", the answer is always "feel it";
  • You keep making the same mistake and it has never come up in a lesson;
  • You become increasingly unwilling to play wrong in that lesson — that is the signal that the chance to be diagnosed is disappearing.
