Genre versus FormTimbre

"Your taste is good" presupposes that taste has a hierarchy. A more accurate line: taste is a biography — a record of what you heard, with whom, and who you wanted to become. Taken apart, its sources are clear.

Three sources

1. Mere exposure: heard often, liked more — much of "liking" is a function of familiarity (hence a new genre needs a few listens to "get in"); 2. Social identity: music labels belonging — "people like me listen to this". Teenage taste is especially shaped by belonging (part of the reminiscence-peak story); 3. Individual differences: appetite for intensity (loud vs soft), openness and so on — same exposure, different results.

Why it looks like "high and low"

Sociologists long ago noted that "high vs low" talk often works as social distinction — what you listen to marks which circle you belong to. Classical vs pop, underground vs mainstream: under the argument sits identity, not aesthetics.

This is not nihilism: deeper listeners do catch more structure, style history and detail — not "higher", but higher resolution.

Taste is plastic: the reassuring part

  • It does not lock in childhood: active listening as an adult genuinely widens the borders;
  • "Not getting it" is not a qualification failure: every unfamiliar genre is strange on the first pass — the normal starting line of exposure;
  • Widening is plain work: three to five different artists in one genre → find its borders with other genres → repeat.

Three lines for explorers

1. Swap "is it good" for "what is it trying to do, and does it land" — judgement sharpens instantly; 2. Concede context: the same piece is two different objects on the metro and in a hall; 3. Audit your own biography: can you say how your taste grew? — if yes, you are already listening actively.

Listen: an "odd, then flavoured" sound

Hear a line built on an unusual interval — strange on pass one, landing by pass two. That is exposure at work.
