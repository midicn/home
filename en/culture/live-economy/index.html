Long before the word "record" existed, musicians already lived off the stage. Live is the industry's oldest income line — and in the last three decades it has moved from supporting role back to co-lead.

Where a show's money comes from

  • Ticketing: the main engine. Price × seats × shows sets a concert's revenue ceiling;
  • Sponsorship: brands paying for exposure;
  • Merchandise: T-shirts, posters, records — for many touring bands, merch is the highest-margin line;
  • Venue splits: a cut of drinks, parking, premium seats.

Cost structure: why "sold out" can still mean broke

A show pays out first: venue, equipment (sound and lights), people (security, staff, logistics), promotion, artist fees — plus ticketing-platform charges. What remains is the actual take.

Hence an industry commonplace: touring is a logistics-and-cost business — a big band tours like moving a factory; a small band tours like elegant budget travel.

Touring economics: why bands live on the road

For many musicians, recorded income is thin and slow; live income is thick and fast. Touring has shifted from promotion tactic to primary living: the album becomes the reason to tour, not the goal.

At scale, this feeds back into songwriting: which songs detonate in an arena, which invite a full-crowd singalong — real considerations at the writing desk.

Prices and scalpers

Ticket prices and resale markets are the live world's most persistent argument. The core tension is simple: pricing power, the legal limits of resale, and "can fans actually get tickets" cannot all be maximised at once. Dynamic pricing, ID checks and purchase limits take turns; the answer is still pending.

Why live cannot be replaced

A paradox: recordings are cheaper, clearer and always available — yet live keeps growing. Because a show sells more than sound: the irreplaceable "same time, same room, same crowd". It is the part of the industry most like a festival — and hardest to digitise.
