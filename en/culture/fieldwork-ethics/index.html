Field recording looks like "set up the machine, press once". The moment you press it, you are doing something with ethical weight. Settle these five questions before you leave home.

One. Who consented?

Recording is not itself a permission. The field's baseline requirement is informed consent:

  • Who agreed? The performer? A community representative with authority?
  • Agreed to what? Academic study? Public playback? Commercial release?
  • Up to which level? In a classroom, online, commercially — these are four different permissions.

And: consent can be withdrawn. People have the right to know the recording exists, where it is, and who can hear it.

Two. Who owns the recording?

  • The performer? The researcher? The community?
  • In many Indigenous cultures, certain songs are held by the community rather than the individual — individual consent is not community consent;
  • Songs traditionally not for outsiders (rites, funerals) cannot simply be published because a performer said yes.

Three. How are money and benefit shared?

  • How much, and on what schedule: a lump sum, or a share of plays/revenue?
  • What comes back: beyond money — books, copies of the recording, teaching gear, a restoration project?
  • Who keeps the master tape? When the researcher leaves, can the community still reach it?

Four. Does your presentation harm?

  • A lament illustrated cheerfully can wound;
  • A dialect labelled "some crude music" is a stigma;
  • Presentation (image, text, translation) is itself interpretation — it can honour or consume.

Five. What did you take, what did you leave?

  • Recording is a one-way extraction: you leave; your material stays.
  • Good fieldwork leaves capability: teaching local young people to record, catalogue, perform;
  • Archives should be two-way: material returns to the community, not locked in a university cabinet.

Three minimum practices

1. Ask before recording — the first sentence should be "may I record?" 2. Agreements beat promises — write it down, including "not for what"; 3. Re-confirm before publishing — in ten years you will forget; the recording will not.

Listen: recorded call and response

Hear a lead-and-response alternation — a product of live labour, fixed by recording into a text that can be cited again and again. The ethics live exactly here.
