China's music market shares an origin with the global story but took its own road: the same media generations, each landing in a distinct local shape.

Distinct stations along the way

  • Tape and CD era: in step with the world, but its circulation reached unusually deep — county-town record shops were once part of the infrastructure;
  • The ringtone era (2000s): a unique "digital distribution ahead of schedule" — phone ringtones brought pay-per-play to China nearly a decade early;
  • Platforms and digital albums (2010s): streaming platforms became the main channel, and the digital album became a ritual purchase — buying a virtual record to back an artist;
  • Variety shows and short video (2010s–20s): music shows carried both star-making and song-pushing; short-video platforms became the first live venue for many songs — a chorus snippet often gets heard before the song.

A decade of legitimisation

Another visible shift happened on the rights side: from piracy-dominant circulation toward platforms buying and normalising licensed content. It reshaped the whole distribution structure — rights fees moved from "barely existent" to "a line item you must negotiate", and created an entire profession: rights operations.

Live and festivals

The past decade saw live shows and festivals grow fast in major cities — a "social consumption" for younger listeners. The pattern matches the global rule: when recorded music gets cheap, live gets expensive.

A reflection: the general meaning of a distinct path

China's route reminds us the industry has more than one script. The order in which channels appear deeply shapes how music is consumed — ringtones pulled pay-per-play forward; short video made "snippet first" a habit.

Listen: the common material of Chinese melody

One modal foundation of Chinese melody is the pentatonic scale — five notes, five positions.
