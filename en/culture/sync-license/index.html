In the trade, setting music to picture has a formal name — sync: music synchronised with the image. Its workflow is clear, and once learned it lasts a career.

(Process outline, not legal advice.)

Step one: decide the layer

  • Using an existing recording → recording layer + work layer (two licences);
  • Re-performing the music → work layer only (but you make the recording);
  • Using public-domain or openly licensed material → check terms; often mere attribution.

Step two: find the owners

MaterialWhom to ask
Words & musicPublisher / collective
RecordingLabel (or the independent artist directly)
Pre-cleared libraryThe platform's subscription licence

Independents are often the easiest to talk to — one email can open the subject: usually a fair fee and a clear scope is all they want.

Step three: negotiate clearly

A sync licence should state: use (series/ad/game), scope (territory, term), exclusivity, fee, credit. Over-broad "use" clauses are the classic trap — you needed short-film background, the contract says "all media, perpetual, worldwide" — that is a price increase for your future self.

Step four: keep records

Agreements, emails, payment receipts — keep the whole chain. Distributors, platforms and clients may ask for proof of clearance at any time; teams that can produce it never lose the beat.

Three common misunderstandings

  • "My platform subscription = the right to soundtrack a video" → entirely different things;
  • "I bought the album = I can put it in my video" → likewise;
  • "I could not find the owner, so I used it" → not finding the owner is a reason to *wait*, not a reason to use (move to openly licensed material instead).

The professional way on a budget

1. Open-licence or royalty-free libraries (compliant and fast); 2. Commission a custom piece (increasingly common, wide price range); 3. Public-domain work + new recording (half the paperwork gone).

Pros never run bare — they simply know the alternatives better.
