Oral and Written — Two Worlds of Transmission

A score preserves notes, not how to make them live

Related

StaffMelody

"Music can be preserved in two ways — on paper, or in people's heads and hands." This gets flattened into an "oral vs written" binary. Reality is closer to two roads running side by side for centuries, each irreplaceable.

What a score keeps, and what it drops

A clean score precisely preserves: pitch, duration, some texture and formal clues.

What it drops (unless annotated at exhausting length):

  • How to attack (legato, pluck, vibrato);
  • How to decorate (vibrato width, when to add flourish);
  • Posture and breath;
  • In what occasion, at what tempo — especially with no metronome mark.

Result: a "strictly from the score" old song often sounds like a different piece.

What oral transmission keeps

Oral transmission seems "imprecise", but it keeps a different set:

  • How phrases and breath are organised (where to inhale, where to push to the peak);
  • On-the-spot judgement — "this time, sing it differently";
  • Tempo and genre choices bound to the occasion;
  • And crucially: how to improvise — decorating a skeleton without collapsing it.

The two routes transmit different things. Comparing them on "accuracy" measures two different chairs with one ruler.

Why oral versions "change"

A common misreading: oral = drift = worse. The actual rule is the opposite:

  • Oral tradition transmits variable pieces — every performance is "this time's" version;
  • Variation is not decay — it is the core mechanism of the art (see folk variants);
  • Written tradition transmits fixed pieces — less variation between players, more stability in the repertoire.

Great music exists in both. The difference is what you want.

Where they meet: variant scores and digital audio

The interesting convergence is under way:

  • Variant scores (one revised copy per player) existed in manuscript times — 18th-century British songbooks are full of such "private versions" (see the second example above);
  • Digital audio sits between: more precise than oral (identical every hearing) yet carrying more than notation (timbres, dynamics, minute timing).

How these three relate is still debated — whether recording will erase the oral "different every time" is itself a fieldwork hot spot (see how recording changes folk music).

A felt sense for learners

If you swing between "play from the score" and "learn from someone" — they are not rival camps. A score gives you skeleton, a person gives you flesh; you need both, only the order differs.
