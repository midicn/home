How Recording Changed Folk Music

A recording fixes one of many versions; that is both a gain and a loss

3 min readUpdated 2026-09-29

ethnomusicologyrecordingtransmission

Related

ImprovisationMelody

Before recording existed, a folk song could be fixed in only two ways: written down, or remembered by enough people. Recording changed the threshold for that — and the consequences.

What recording does first: fix what used to vary

Return to the dataset: the first three examples are one tune recorded in three different sources. Before recording, they travelled through manuscripts and through people's memories, each unaware of the others.

What recording does is simple: it freezes one performance into an object that can be replayed without end.

One consequence follows directly — a variant gains a chance to outweigh the others.

  • A fieldworker brings a recorder and can usually capture only the one occasion they happened to encounter;
  • that occasion is preserved and becomes "a version";
  • everyone who hears it later assumes it is the song;
  • and versions that were never recorded may simply stop being sung.

One fixed version won — but it won by accident.

But the loss is not one-directional

A common conclusion runs: recording destroyed folk music. That is only half right.

Recording also solved real old problems:

  • Broken transmission: without recording, a song died with its last singer. Recording at least leaves one copyable sample;
  • Threshold of entry: learning a song used to require being present, in a teacher–student relationship. Recording made self-study possible;
  • An internal standard: in oral tradition "correct" is judged by the community. In the recording era, "correct" often gets mistaken for "sounds like that record" — a real drift, but it also gives more people something to hold on to.

The real loss is not "one fewer variant"

Worth stating precisely, because it is often stated wrongly:

The loss is not that recording "fixed a version" — it is that "being fixed" quietly became the goal of transmission.

In oral tradition, whether a version is worth keeping was judged by the people who used it. In the recording era that judgement quietly moves to the people who listen — and listeners do not join the next round.

What emerges is not "recording is worse than oral", but two different directions of continuity:

  • Oral: continues the capacity to do the thing (next time, something new can be produced);
  • Recording: continues the result (this time, it was like this).

Capacity can be lost; results cannot. That is where the weight actually falls.

A phenomenon you can check yourself

Listen to the three examples above in sequence and a fairly precise impression forms:

  • The three share a very similar skeleton (so they are variants of one thing);
  • Their details differ stably (so the divergence is real, not noise);
  • and the "original tune" you now hear is an impression formed from meeting all three — not any single record.

That last point is worth noticing: your "original" is a product of three encounters, not a recording.

How to use recordings

A workable stance is to hold two things apart:

  • As reference: listen for structure, timbre, context;
  • As variant: notice where it differs from other versions — that is not a flaw, it is information.

What this means for recording ethics: the captured occasion is not the correct version, it is one occasion. This site's requirements on fieldwork are set out in fieldwork ethics.
