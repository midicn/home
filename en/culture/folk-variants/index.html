"Which is the original version of this song?" — for folk music that question is usually unanswerable, and asking it already reveals a category error.

Variation is not decay

The default picture is: one original author writes the original → copying gradually distorts it → what we see now is a blurred shadow.

What is actually common in folk music: there was never an original, only a set of versions that are not quite identical to each other. Each is somebody's music, from somewhere, and stands on its own.

Drift requires an "original" as reference. Folk music has no such reference.

Why every place differs

Because folk music travels through people, and people differ:

  • Different voices → a different highest reachable note → the tune shifts with it;
  • Different instruments or modes → a different set of playable notes → the melody gets bent onto what can be played;
  • Different occasions → a wedding wants energy, a funeral wants restraint; the same tune wears two faces;
  • Words must fit the mouth → change the words, or change the tune, or change to another tune entirely.

So "singing out of tune" is often not a mistake but a necessary condition.

Sometimes variation converges — the opposite direction

A common misreading: more contact means more chaos.

In practice the opposite often happens: heavy contact pulls variants toward "the one version everybody can sing". The reason is practical — a tune only three people in one village know dies out when they do; a tune anyone can hum half of is the one likely to survive.

So two forces act at once in folk music:

  • Divergence — each singer adjusts to their own voice and occasion;
  • Convergence — to be taken up by more people, simplifying, dropping what is awkward.

A "standard version" is usually the product of convergence, not the original state.

A phenomenon you can verify yourself

This site's dataset holds many records under the same tune name from different sources. Examples one, two and three above are one tune preserved once in each of four sources. Listen three times in a row:

  • From the second pass you will hear some things identical, some not;
  • and the places that differ differ the same way every time — not random noise, but stable divergence.

Stable divergence is what a variant is.

The trouble with "authentic"

This raises a practical ethical problem: once a folk song is arranged, recorded and printed in a textbook, the question "how should it be sung" hardens — and the hardened version often comes from whichever person the fieldworker happened to meet.

Respecting a tradition does not mean accepting one collector's selection as the sole standard. This has its own discussion in the site's fieldwork ethics page.
