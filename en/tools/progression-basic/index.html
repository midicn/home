Home›Three Commonest Progressions — Why They Sound Familiar

Below are three progressions you have almost certainly heard somewhere. This page does not scold them for being clichés; it argues that they are the three cheapest ways of resolving.

One: I – IV – V – I (the most basic loop)

This one's job is to return you to the start. It creates nothing new; it only confirms that you are still where you were.

Two: I – V – vi – IV (the most colourful)

This one's job is to move forward without getting lost. The vi and the IV keep it from settling on the tonic, yet both remain inside the major key.

Three: i – VI – III – VII (the loop in a minor key)

This is progression two in a minor key. The difference: the minor mode replaces VI / III / VII with major chords.

Why these three sound familiar

Three reasons, in order of importance.

1. They are the cheapest

Every step is the most natural motion available:

  • I → IV → V → I: three chords in the same function, maximally settled;
  • I → V: dominant function toward tonic, maximally directional;
  • I → vi / IV: connectable with no inversions at all.

Cheap means needing no explanation — and that is precisely what "sounds familiar" is.

2. Their range is tight

Above, the chords sit essentially within one octave. That means the ear is not pulled apart by wide spans, so it stays concentrated.

3. Their mood is neutral-to-bright

None of them contains a diminished triad or a pile of sevenths. So they sound like steady forward motion, without fear or suspense.

If you want drama, these three are not the answer — see the piece on cadences next.

An experiment you can run yourself

Replace the final I in progression one with V.

You get the feeling of stopping half-way — which is exactly what the page on cadences is about. The three progressions are the road; that last chord is the stop.

An honest note

This page gives three, but there are thousands of progressions. These three are foundational not because they are best but because they are cheapest.

Practical advice when writing: start from these three (they are very hard to get wrong), and once your hands are steady, start replacing them — and when you do, you will know exactly what you are changing.
