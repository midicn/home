How to PractiseMetronome and BPMJohann Sebastian Bach

"How long should I practise each day?" has a counter-intuitive answer: forget duration, manage frequency first.

Twenty minutes daily beats a two-hour weekend session. The reason is simple: skills are kept alive by repetition, and after a three-day gap, the first half of every session is spent finding your hands again.

What a twenty-minute session looks like

A structure beginners can copy directly:

  • 3–5 minutes of warm-up: scales, long tones, open strings, breathing — wake the body up.
  • 10 minutes on the main item: one concrete problem for today. "The hand-change in bar three without a thump" is a goal; "practising" is not.
  • 5 minutes of play: a piece you love, no corrections allowed. These five minutes are why you will sit down again tomorrow.

What counts as practice — and what does not

Playing a piece through once is not practice. Practice = find the snag + narrow the range + repeat + repeat differently.

One test: when you close the lid, can you say what you solved today? If yes, those twenty minutes were high-quality ones.

Busy days: a five-minute floor

Some nights work runs late. Two moves keep the habit alive:

1. Set a five-minute floor: warm-up plus one bar, and the day counts. The floor exists to keep the chain unbroken. 2. Hang practice on an existing habit: after dinner, before brushing teeth — so "when to practise" is not a fresh decision every day.

If a day slips entirely, let it be a deliberate rest (I know I skipped; tomorrow I resume) rather than a "forgotten" one — forgetting, repeated a few times, is how habits end.

Soreness, pain, tiredness

  • Soreness is normal training feedback; pain is a stop signal — stop at once.
  • Every twenty to thirty minutes, stand up and move your wrists and shoulders.
  • Short and regular beats long and forced: repetition under fatigue engraves the mistakes, not the music.

Listen: the slow, steady beat

Most moments of "I can't get through this" happen when speed runs away with you. Take the metronome and start slow — sixty beats a minute, every beat exactly where it belongs.

The metronome is not a supervisor but a measuring tool: learn your real tempo first, then decide how fast to practise.
