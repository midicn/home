"Do I have talent?" Almost everyone asks it on day one. It looks like honest self-assessment; in fact it is a double trap.

Trap one: there is no agreed measurement

"Talent" is not something you can measure. Worse, different people mean entirely different things by it:

  • some mean perfect pitch (naming a note from hearing it alone);
  • some mean rhythmic sense (how the body answers a beat);
  • some mean memory (holding on to a melody you heard);
  • some mean hand speed (sheer agility).

A word that is undefined and uncalibrated cannot answer "how much do I have?" Every "assessment" you receive is really somebody measuring you with a random ruler.

Trap two: the answer changes nothing

Suppose someone could tell you reliably: "You have talent." What do you do today? Practise.

Suppose the answer were "none". If you love music, what do you do today? Practise.

An answer that changes no action costs you mainly the time spent asking. That is the trap: it disguises itself as caution, but it works like procrastination.

Early speed is not talent; endurance is not talent either

Look closely at "fast learners" and a pattern appears: early speed usually comes from exposure — lots of listening, an instrument at home, a childhood of touching keys. That is an experience advantage, not a talent advantage. And those who last share something plainer — they cannot stop: practice is wired into their week, and progress itself becomes the reward.

Musical ability is also multidimensional: ear, motion, memory, taste, expression each develop on their own schedule. Being average in one place often means a fresh start in another — which is another reason "talent tests" measure very little.

Three replacement questions

1. "Can I reliably give this twenty minutes a day?" If yes, you have everything needed to learn music. 2. "What do I want music to give me?" The answer picks your path. 3. "How do I make the tenth session different from the first?" This is where the real secret of good students lives.

Listen: evidence that it can be trained

Below is a major third — the distance between two notes. At first it may mean nothing; carry it around for a week of ear practice and it will start to have a flavour. That change is direct evidence that ability responds to repetition, not to talent.
