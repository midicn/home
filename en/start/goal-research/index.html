Want to Study Music Seriously?

Start from one answerable question — serious study needs no university

2 min readUpdated 2026-09-28

"Studying music" sounds like a professor's job. In fact it starts small: turn what you are curious about into a question that can be answered.

What music research looks like

  • Historical musicology: how a manuscript was written, how an arrangement travelled, what happened at a premiere;
  • Ethnomusicology: a place, a genre, a people — when music is used and why that way;
  • Theory and analysis: why a piece is written as it is, and what a rewrite would do;
  • Psychology and cognition: how people hear, remember, and get moved;
  • Digital humanities: studying music at scale — catalogues, versions and transmission as data.

A path for amateurs (three moves)

1. Pick a question small enough to finish: "which versions of this song exist in China" is finishable; "the study of Chinese folk song" is not. 2. Find primary material: archives, datasets, recordings, scores. Every record in an open archive is potential raw material. 3. Check what others have done: you cannot read everything, but you can find the nearest survey and key studies — that decides whether your question is new or already settled.

A small demonstration: variant study

Two rows of data are enough. The same song, "Scarborough Fair", sits in one catalogue in two versions — note the differences first, then ask why. The essence of research is that plain.

Ethics and norms

  • Field recording is research on people — seek consent, explain the purpose, respect the community's say over the recordings;
  • Cite properly: attribute ideas and data honestly;
  • Check licences: before reusing scores or recordings, confirm permission — open licences (like the tiers in the dataset) smooth the way, but every item still needs a look.

The material at hand is bigger than you think

Every example on this site opens a "details" link showing its source, licence and identifiers — structured information is digital humanities' best first material: international archives, living-tradition collections, performance datasets, all sitting behind these entries.

Listen: a pentatonic scale — the sound of choosing

Different cultures pick different favourites out of twelve notes — the traditional Chinese five-note scale is a classic selection. Research often begins with "why these, and not the others".
