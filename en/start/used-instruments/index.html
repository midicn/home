Classical GuitarViolinConcert Pitch

Musical instruments are one of the most normal second-hand categories: they hold value well, many owners upgrade within a year or two, and older instruments are often underpriced. But "cheap" does not automatically become "a good deal" — a checklist does that.

What suits buying used

  • Good fit: simple, standardised instruments — guitars, digital pianos, most winds, violins (plenty of stock).
  • Care needed: instruments dominated by wear parts (reeds), ageing electronics (old synths), and anything needing expert appraisal (old acoustic pianos — bring a tuner).

Six moves anyone can make

1. Look in daylight: flaws cannot hide in good light. 2. Play your try-out pieces (three below): listen for — any buzz, even volume across notes, harsh highs. 3. Check for physical damage: on guitars, sight down the neck and check the bridge; on winds, dents and hardened pads; on strings, cracks (hold against the light). 4. Read the wear: fret dents, key wear, the patina — how long it served is more honest than the seller's story. 5. Verify the serial number: match it against receipts; walk away from cheap-without-papers (it may be stolen). 6. Anchor the price: look up recent sold prices for the same model, compare three — never anchor on "new" price.

Preparing "try-out pieces"

Bring three to the viewing (even as phone recordings):

  • a solo passage — exposes intonation and action;
  • a study — tests evenness across the range;
  • music you love — it will tell you whether you really want to take it home.

Two "don'ts" at the transaction

  • Do not pay untested (not even same-city delivery);
  • Do not pay for "stories" (whose teacher owned it does not matter — only condition and price).
