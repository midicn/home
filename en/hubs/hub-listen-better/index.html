I Want to Understand Music Better

Understanding music is not sharper ears but knowing what to listen for

2 min readUpdated 2026-11-02

hublisteningear training

"I want to understand music better" is usually read as "my ears need to get sharper". A more useful view: understanding music equals knowing what to listen for. This page gives you an actionable checklist and eighteen clickable tools.

What you are trying to do here

First work out which kind of "understand" you want:

  • hearing it (is this a triad) — ear training; there is a full set of tools here;
  • understanding it (what is it doing, why is it written this way) — concepts, mostly text;
  • remembering it (still recalling it later) — this is memory, not hearing.

The three are trained completely differently. The piece on music and memory covers the third properly.

Where to read: by the kind of understanding

One: hearing it — practise with the tools

All eighteen tools are designed around one executable judgement each. Start with these three:

  • What an interval is: eight distances at once — build "an interval is a distance, not a height"
  • Major and minor thirds: one word apart — the hardest pair, and the one most worth practising
  • Interval identification drill — which layer you stall at

The full list of eighteen is in the tools index, grouped as intervals, scales, chords, progressions, rhythm, keys and timbre.

Two: understanding it — the text is in the culture section

  • How music triggers emotion: expectation and resolution — mechanism, not mystique
  • Why the major scale sounds happy: explanation and dispute — four explanations of one question
  • Music and memory: why old songs carry memory — how music unlocks recall
  • Rhythm and body: why we want to move — the body arrives before the ear
  • The problem with the term world music — know the frame before you listen

Three: hearing it whole — know the structure first

This layer is most often skipped, and it decides what you are able to hear at all.

  • Musical form — learn the skeleton of a piece first
  • Music history — context by period, region and style
  • Onsite: the instrument guide — what timbre is and where it comes from

An executable three-step method

If you only have half an hour and no idea where to start:

1. Listen to a whole piece once, without analysing anything (this step is most often skipped, and it decides whether any later analysis is enjoyable) 2. Look up its formal structure (the form chapters offsite, on the theory site) 3. Go back and listen again with that structure in mind, focusing on one thing only (for instance: "what key is the second section in?")

Step 1 cannot be skipped. Analysing the score without ever hearing the piece means you are analysing notation, not music.

Keep listening

Use music history for context and the glossary for terms. The listening tools are not a complete graded dictation course. Revisit one specific question in a piece to compare what you hear.
