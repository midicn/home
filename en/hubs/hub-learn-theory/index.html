I Want to Understand Music Theory

Theory is not a pile of terms — it is three layers: how to notate it, why it holds, how to use it

1 min read0 characters页面Updated 2026-10-03

hubtheory

The most common way to fail at music theory is to treat it as a list of terms to memorise. It is really three layers, and each is learned differently.

What you are trying to do here

First identify which layer you are after:

What you needLayerWhere
"what is this mark on the page"notationtheo + glossary
"why a sharp here and not a flat"principletheo's key-signature and harmony parts
"I know it — now what"usagethe culture pages and tools

Where to read: by layer

Layer one: notation (how to read it)

The core of this layer is lookup, not study.

  • Offsite: music theory — over 150 entries, starting from note names and the staff
  • Offsite: glossary — term lookup (but short of its plan, see below)
  • Onsite: metre: why 3/4 and 4/4 sound different — the most intuitive explanation of this layer

Layer two: principle (why it holds)

This is the only part of theory that genuinely requires understanding.

  • Offsite: music theory — the chapters on key signatures, intervals and progressions
  • Onsite: the circle of fifths — an interactive map of the key-signature system
  • Onsite: modes: one frame, seven home bases — why one set of pitches carries seven colours

Layer three: usage (how to apply it)

This layer is what turns knowledge into music.

  • Onsite: the three commonest progressions — the three cheapest
  • Onsite: cadences: where the feeling of stopping comes from — why some endings "land"
  • Onsite: suspensions — the second before it resolves
  • Onsite: folk variants: there is no "original" — where principle loosens in folk song

One line through all three layers

If you want to see notation → principle → usage connected in one place, start with why the major scale sounds happy. It starts from the simplest question, climbs one layer at a time, and includes the dispute about its explanation.

What is not built yet

Honestly, three gaps:

1. The glossary is short of its plan — lookup is what this layer needs most, and it is still a skeleton. This is the most practical obstacle on this route 2. The theory site has no learning path — it is 150 parallel entries with no indication of "read these ten first" 3. No entry point showing how the terms derive from each other — each entry explains itself and not its neighbours

I consider point two a genuine gap: a 150-entry parallel knowledge site is fine for those who already understand the subject, and equivalent to no order at all for a beginner.
