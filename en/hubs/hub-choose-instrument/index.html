I Want to Learn an Instrument

Do not ask which instrument is best; ask what you are willing to endure

Most advice about choosing an instrument talks about "which one is best". That question has no answer. The one that does is: what are you willing to endure.

What you are trying to do here

Choosing an instrument takes three decisions, and the order matters:

1. What you are willing to endure (hand shape, stamina, noise, money) 2. How long you can practise (this decides how many options elimination removes) 3. What kind of music you want (this decides style, not instrument)

Reverse the order and you will buy the wrong thing. Most people start at step 3 and discover afterwards that step 1 was not passable.

Where to read: three layers

Layer one: eliminate (by your body)

This layer is hard; willpower cannot bypass it.

  • Onsite: budget and a first instrument — how to work out the money constraint
  • Onsite: four claims to be wary of — errors in common recommendations
  • Offsite: the instrument guide — every instrument has a cross-section and a range chart; check first whether your hands cannot do it

On noise, an often-overlooked point on this site: practice and the brain notes that equipment and environment constraints affect long-term practice more than the choice of instrument itself.

Layer two: eliminate by your available time

  • Onsite: how long should I practise each day? — frequency beats duration
  • Onsite: theory first, or instrument first? — they can run in parallel
  • Onsite: piano or keyboard? — one concrete comparison

The practical role of this layer is elimination. If you have twenty minutes a day, the instruments that demand a fixed daily slot get removed by themselves.

Layer three: choose a style, not an instrument

This layer decides what you practise, not what you buy.

  • Onsite: what practice research tells us — the evidence on practice structure
  • Onsite: practice and the brain — the real costs of long-term practice
  • Onsite: adult music education — a completely different design from children's classes

An action you can take yourself

Open the instrument guide and pick three instruments at random; look at how they produce sound. The instrument site has cross-section diagrams and range charts for over 140 instruments, each with a timbre audition.

If looking at a whole family leaves you with no urge to try it, that family can be ruled out — whatever anyone recommends.

What is not built yet

Three honest notes:

1. The instrument site is organised by instrument family, not by "who it suits" — it answers "what is this instrument", not "should I learn it" 2. There is no side-by-side comparison table — you have to hop across 140-odd pages yourself 3. The adult/child difference appears only in two culture articles — the instrument site itself has no choosing-an-instrument guide for adults

Point three is a genuine gap. The piece on adult music education explains how to teach adults, but nothing systematically covers how an adult should choose.
